# GL-133: what the craft literature says makes a poster/wall-art design good

`gate:taste` — findings below are gathered for the owner's read, not a verdict.
Scope is the general craft of poster/wall-art composition, colour, and visual
balance — **not** an audit of what this pipeline currently generates. That
audit is #134's job, once this row picks which findings apply.

Sources are web design-education material (Smashing Magazine, Design Shack,
NN/g, UXPin, Domestika, etc. — see citations per finding) plus current
POD/wall-art market reporting for the sell-side findings. No book was
fetchable in full text during this pass; several hits named books/authors
(da Vinci, Dalí) only in passing, not as a citable source — flagged where that
happened.

## Findings

### F1 — Visual hierarchy needs 2+ reinforcing signals, not one
Size is the single strongest hierarchy tool, but the sources are explicit that
relying on size (or any one tool) alone produces a "flat, one-dimensional"
result — the effective pattern combines size, colour/contrast, and placement
so multiple signals agree on what matters most.
Source: [Visual Hierarchy in Design: A Complete Guide](https://madegooddesigns.com/visual-hierarchy/), [12 Poster Design Principles That Actually Work](https://madegooddesigns.com/poster-design-principles/)

### F2 — Posters are read at a distance / in motion / peripherally
Poster composition literature treats the viewing context as a hard constraint,
distinct from screen or page design: a poster is often seen from across a
room, in someone's peripheral vision, or while they're moving past it. Every
hierarchy and contrast choice is downstream of that constraint.
Source: [Design Theory: Visual Hierarchy and Poster Design](https://medium.com/@kgographics/design-theory-visual-hierarchy-and-poster-design-994d113f0535), [The Graphic Design Principles Behind Effective Posters](https://posters.org/2024/06/03/graphic-design-principles/)
**Applicability note:** this pipeline's output is wall art meant to be looked
at up close and lived with, not a promotional poster glanced at from across a
street — the "read at a glance" framing this literature optimizes for may not
transfer directly. Worth weighing explicitly in #134 rather than importing
wholesale.

### F3 — Limit the palette to 2–3 main hues
Multiple sources converge on the same number: two or three carefully chosen
hues carries more visual punch than a wide palette, because a smaller palette
reduces what the viewer has to parse and reinforces contrast/hierarchy rather
than competing with it.
Source: [Color Theory: Brief Guide For Designers](https://blog.tubikstudio.com/color-theory-brief-guide-for-designers/), [Color Rules of Thumb](https://www.writedesignonline.com/resources/design/rules/color.html)

### F4 — Named colour-ratio conventions: 60-30-10 vs. 50-25-25
For a 3-hue palette, a 60/30/10 split (dominant/secondary/accent) reads as
clear hierarchy — called out as "especially helpful when designing posters" —
while a more even 50/25/25 split reads as more dynamic/energetic but with
less clear hierarchy. Both are named conventions, not a single "correct" ratio.
Source: [Color Theory: Brief Guide For Designers](https://blog.tubikstudio.com/color-theory-brief-guide-for-designers/)

### F5 — Complementary colour pairs create energy; use with restraint
Opposite hues on the colour wheel create dynamic contrast and immediate
appeal, but every source pairs this with a restraint warning: overused
contrast (colour or otherwise) reads as chaotic and undercuts the
composition rather than strengthening it.
Source: [Visual Contrast Poster Design: Color & Tone Impact](https://www.sprakdesign.com/visual-contrast-in-poster-design/)

### F6 — Asymmetric balance reads as more dynamic than symmetric
Symmetrical balance is stable but can read as static/uninteresting on its
own. Asymmetric balance — uneven visual weight that still nets out to
equilibrium via contrast, scale, colour weight, and negative space — is
named as the more engaging default for compositions that want to feel alive
rather than purely calm.
Source: [Symmetry vs. Asymmetry in Design (UXPin)](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/symmetry-vs-asymmetry-in-design/), [Design Principles: Compositional, Symmetrical And Asymmetrical Balance (Smashing Magazine)](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2015/06/design-principles-compositional-balance-symmetry-asymmetry/)

### F7 — Negative space is an active compositional tool, not leftover area
Across every source touching balance or minimalism, negative space is framed
as something the designer *places*, not empty space that's merely tolerated.
Ample negative space around one element is itself how asymmetric balance and
a clear focal point get built — the emptiness is doing work, not absent of it.
Source: [Graphic Design Principles: Balance and White Space](https://blog.thenounproject.com/graphic-design-principles-balance-and-white-space/), [50 Examples of Negative Space Design (Canva)](https://www.canva.com/learn/negative-space/)

### F8 — Rule of thirds: put the single focal point off-centre, at an intersection
When a composition has one point of interest, the convention is to place it
at one of the four intersections of a 3×3 grid rather than dead-centre — the
rule-of-thirds "strength points." Sources describe this as the accessible,
easy-to-apply version of the golden ratio, not a separate/competing rule.
Source: [The Rule of Thirds in Art](https://thevirtualinstructor.com/blog/the-rule-of-thirds-in-art), [What Is and How to Apply the Rule of Thirds in Illustration (Domestika)](https://www.domestika.org/en/blog/4703-what-is-and-how-to-apply-the-rule-of-thirds-in-illustration)

### F9 — Golden ratio (1:1.618) named as the "ideal" compositional proportion
Named across sources as historically associated with da Vinci, Michelangelo,
and Dalí — cited in passing by the design-blog sources, not verified here
against a primary art-history text, so treat the attribution as secondhand.
The usable takeaway independent of the history claim: a spiral/rectangle
proportion near 1:1.618 is a stronger, harder-to-apply version of the same
"don't centre your one focal point" idea as the rule of thirds.
Source: [Composition Rules: Rule of Thirds, Golden Ratio (Lovart Academy)](https://blogs.lovart.ai/composition-rules-design-rule-of-thirds-golden-ratio.html)

### F10 (market/sell-side) — earthy, muted, low-saturation palettes lead current wall-art demand
2026 wall-art market reporting converges on warm neutrals (beige, taupe,
clay, olive, sage) as the dominant base palette, with saturated colour used
sparingly as accent rather than as the base. This is a market-trend claim,
not a craft-theory claim — it's about what's currently selling, and it will
date faster than F1–F9.
Source: [Wall Art Trends 2026 (Mixtiles)](https://www.mixtiles.com/blog/wall-art-home-decor/wall-art-trends), [COLOR TRENDS 2026 (Emily Henderson)](https://stylebyemilyhenderson.com/color-trends-2026)

### F11 (market/sell-side) — sparse, single-subject compositions are named as currently dominant
Multiple wall-art-market sources independently name "visual calm" — sparse
composition, simple line work, one clear subject not competing for
attention — as the currently dominant taste, alongside oversized single
statement pieces over cluttered multi-element arrangements.
Source: [Wall Art Trends 2026 (Mixtiles)](https://www.mixtiles.com/blog/wall-art-home-decor/wall-art-trends), [Wall Art Trends 2026: 8 Best-Selling Ideas for POD Sellers (Merchize)](https://merchize.com/wall-art-trends/)

## Shortlist — findings that look applicable to this pipeline

Phrased so #134 can turn accepted ones into execution rows.

- **F1 + F8** (multi-signal hierarchy + off-centre focal point) look directly
  applicable to `art_brief.py`'s composition guidance for single-subject
  designs — the brief template already asks for a density/coverage clause and
  a focal-hierarchy subject; a rule-of-thirds placement clause is a small,
  concrete addition if #134 wants one.
- **F3 + F4** (2–3 hue palette, named ratio) map closely to the brief
  template's existing "2–4 named accent colors from exactly one palette
  family" rule (`docs/deep-research-briefing-template.md` point 7) — mostly
  confirms current practice rather than changing it; F4's 60/30/10 vs.
  50/25/25 language could sharpen the *ratio* guidance, which the current
  brief field doesn't specify.
- **F6 + F7** (asymmetric balance built from negative space) map to the
  brief template's existing bottom-edge-grounding and backdrop-device
  guidance (points 5–6) — largely already-encoded, worth a read to confirm
  no gap.
- **F10 + F11 are market-trend claims, not craft claims** — flag for #134 to
  decide whether they belong in niche/palette selection (`pipeline/research.py`)
  rather than in composition rules, since they'll go stale independently of
  F1–F9 and shouldn't be baked into a fixed prompt template the way the
  craft-theory findings could be.
- Findings **not** shortlisted: F2 (poster-at-a-glance framing may not
  transfer to wall art meant for close, sustained viewing — needs an explicit
  owner call, not an assumed import) and F9 (golden ratio — F8's rule of
  thirds already covers the same idea more simply; adding both would be
  redundant guidance for a text prompt).

## Owner's read (2026-09-02, `/interview`, three rounds)

The owner read the findings above and routed them. Recorded here so a later
session weighs the decision rather than re-deriving it; the execution detail
lives on #134.

**Framing.** This is a **craft upgrade, not a defect fix** — no observed
badly-composed render prompted it. That makes #134 an experiment row, and
"the next batch looks fine" its real acceptance.

**F8 accepted, and corrected.** The original write-up misread the rule of
thirds as off-centre on *both* axes, which would have fought field 3's
existing "centered" option. The owner's correction: rule of thirds means
off-centre on **one** axis. In a 2:3 portrait that means horizontally
centred is fine, with the subject sitting slightly **above or below the
vertical midpoint** rather than dead-centre. That is a refinement of field 3,
not a new field — it folds into field 3's existing position sentence at a
cost of roughly eight words, rather than becoming a field 8.

**F1 rejected.** Multi-signal hierarchy would have spent brief words to state
a third signal (colour/contrast agreeing with size) that the image model may
not honour anyway. F8's vertical offset carries this row alone.

**The scarce resource is the brief's 60/75-word output ceiling, not the
instruction template's length.** Haiku reads a long template cheaply; the
ceiling is what forces new clauses to compress existing ones, and per
`art_brief.py`'s own prompt-craft note (lines 96–113) the detail FLUX drops
first is exactly the small stuff FM-7/FM-9 fought three rounds to secure.
The ceiling **does not move** — F8 folds into field 3 instead.

**F4 accepted as prose only.** A colour-ratio (60/30/10) clause goes in the
brief wording with no lint check behind it; colour proportion can't be
checked without measuring the rendered image, and the owner's review is the
enforcement ("looks good y/n").

**F10 + F11 accepted as confirmation, no action.** They describe what fields 2
and 7 already encode. Note the interaction with GL-131 (#131): the pipeline
was just found over-pinned to the nature/earth-tone neighbourhood (100% of
`event_lookahead`), so pushing *further* toward sage-and-calm on the strength
of a market-trend finding would deepen a defect another row exists to fix.
Confirmed, deliberately not acted on.

**F2, F3, F6, F7, F9** — unchanged from the shortlist above: F2 and F9 not
shortlisted, F3/F6/F7 already encoded.
